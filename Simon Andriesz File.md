## HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT

Before we start, let me give you the three tools this whole file is built on. They matter because the subject matter — a dead whistleblower, a cabinet secretary, the Epstein case — is the single most contaminated information environment in modern public life, and the only way through it is discipline.

**Tool one: the tier system.** Every factual claim in this document carries a tag that tells you how well it is supported.

- **[S]** means a primary document — a government record, a court filing, a sworn transcript, an official report, a deed. Things you could go look at yourself.
- **[A]** means reporting by a major outlet that quotes or links those documents — the BBC, the Associated Press, Forbes, Reuters, The Atlantic.
- **[B]** means credible reporting that paraphrases without full sourcing, or specialist and trade press.
- **[C]** means a single-source or contested claim. It is always flagged as such, and it is kept in the document anyway — because even a false claim is evidence of something: of pressure, of incentives, of fog.

Where finer distinctions matter, three labels appear inside the text: **T1** for a documented fact, **T2** for an attributed claim or clearly labeled analysis, and **T3** for speculation or debunked material retained because it is part of the record.

**Tool two: the categories.** Every person and event in this document is filed by kind — the whistleblower's own story, the regulators, the sworn testimony, the money, the deaths, the institutions. The categories matter because they prevent the single fatal error of this subject: treating a ruled suicide, an unverified tip, and a debunked rumor as the same kind of thing.

**Tool three: the rule.** Collect clues, don't adopt theories. Where a ruling is disputed, both the ruling and the dispute are presented with their documents. Where a claim collapsed, the collapse is documented with the same care as the claim. Where something cannot be known from the public record, the document says so and names what would settle it. Nothing is overstated, because the dead cannot correct the record and the living can.

One more thing before we begin. This file is written to be read by someone who has never heard of any of these people. Every acronym is unpacked the first time it appears. Every institution is explained. Every document is walked through slowly. If you already know the material, forgive the patience; the person this was written for does not.

# PART I — THE MAN

## Chapter 1: Who Simon Andriesz Was

Simon Andriesz was born in October 1968, and he died on September 23, 2026, at the age of fifty-seven. [A] He was British, and he was a banker — not a famous one, not a wealthy one by the standards of the world he worked in, but a serious one. By his own account, given to the FBI, he spent thirty-two years in the securities business. [S]

His career ran through the engine room of a particular kind of Wall Street and City of London firm. From 2012 to 2017 he worked for BGC Partners, a brokerage spun out of Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm run by Howard Lutnick. [S] From 2012 to 2015 he was based in London; in 2015 he transferred to New York, where he ran the global branches of his unit from the New York office. He held what is called a Series 30 license — a qualification that meant he supervised regulatory reporting, the filing of the firm's trades and finances with the authorities. He described himself to the FBI as holding "the most senior position in his unit," managing fifty people. [S — the FBI's own write-up of his February 16, 2021 interview, which we will walk through document by document later in this file.]

A word about what kind of firm BGC and Cantor are, because it matters to everything that follows. A brokerage like BGC makes its money in the gaps — the fractions of a second and fractions of a cent between what a buyer will pay and what a seller will accept, multiplied across enormous volumes of trades. The money flows through pools — commission pools, trading pools — and the people who supervise the reporting of those flows, people like Andriesz, are the ones who see the whole machine at once. They sign the documents. They are the point where the firm's internal reality meets its external paperwork. When something is wrong with the plumbing, they are usually the first to know.

Andriesz's own family remembered him as a man who could not look away. After his death, his family released a statement: "When Simon believed something was wrong, he found it extraordinarily difficult simply to look the other way. That fundamental honesty and courage shaped his life. We are completely devastated." [A — The Banker, October 26, 2026.] He told the BBC, in the one broadcast interview he gave, "It's in my DNA to fight for justice." [A]

He was not a rich man at the end. He told a public meeting in July 2026 — weeks before he died — "I've lost all my money, my wife and I are separated, and I'm in danger of having my home repossessed... Speaking out has devastated my life." [A — The Banker.] In 2015 he had suffered a heart attack at his desk, an event he described in terms that stuck with everyone who heard it: colleagues nearby, he said, did nothing. [B] He had cardiac and trauma complications afterward, spent time in a trauma crisis center earlier in 2026, and was, by the documented record, under severe and sustained strain in his final years — dissociative episodes, multiple hospitalizations. [A — Forbes, BBC.] All of this matters later, and we will return to it in its proper place, because it is the honest record: a man with documented psychological collapse, caused, he said, by the retaliation he was whistleblowing about.

He lived, in his final period, in Cornwall, in the far southwest of England. [A — Forbes, the Daily Beast.] He died in Thailand.

That is the man. Now, what did he say he found?

## Chapter 2: What He Reported

Andriesz's allegations came in two families, and keeping them separate is essential, because they were treated differently by every institution that received them and they are supported differently by the record.

**Family one: financial misconduct at BGC and Cantor Fitzgerald.** He reported — internally first, then to regulators in two countries — that he had witnessed financial irregularities. In his later, formal accounts these included specific claims: that "rope traders" (brokerage jargon for the people executing trades) were gaining access to BGC's electronic trading platform from their homes; that a co-head of brokerage could override controls that were supposed to prevent manipulation; and that, to cover losses, people inside the company "tried to rob the pool of trading funds" — diverting money that belonged to the brokers' commission pools to make the firm's numbers look whole, which, as he explained it to the FBI, "materialized the losses and the brokers were being robbed of revenue." [S — the FBI's FD-302, which we will read in full in Part III.]

He also described what he called a global Ponzi-style share scheme: employees were required, he said, to pay ten percent of their salary and bonus into a company share scheme in which they never became vested; they were told they could sell the shares only if they signed on for four more years; they were given no voting rights; and the share price was dropping. This, he said, involved more than 3,500 employees. [S — the same FD-302.]

He told the FBI that Cantor Fitzgerald's famous charity day — the annual event where the firm donates a day's trading revenue, a day wrapped in the memory of September 11 — was "fraudulent." [S] This claim deserves a moment, because charity day is the moral crown of the Cantor story: the firm's 658 employees died on 9/11, and the charity event is presented as their legacy. To call it fraudulent is to accuse the firm of monetizing its own sacred day. We will see, later in this file, that a Senate subcommittee report and a Senate committee process examined exactly the claims that grew out of this territory — and we will see precisely what was and was not substantiated.

**Family two: Howard Lutnick, Jeffrey Epstein, and the links between them.** This is the family of claims that eventually put Andriesz on the BBC and in front of the House Oversight Committee's orbit. He told the FBI, and later the BBC and members of the U.S. Congress, that he had found documentary evidence of financial and business ties between Howard Lutnick and Jeffrey Epstein that Lutnick had not disclosed — ties that mattered because Lutnick became the United States Secretary of Commerce in February 2025, and because Epstein was, by then, the most notorious sex offender in the country.

The specific claims in this family, as recorded by the FBI in October 2020 and February 2021:

- That Lutnick "ran everything" at his firms and "instructed all of the fraud being committed," and that he "used philanthropy as a smokescreen for illegal activity." [S — recorded as allegations.]
- That Lutnick and Epstein were neighbors, and that "Epstein sold Lutnick a home for $10 which was then sold for millions." [S — recorded as an allegation. As we will see in Part VIII, this one is checkable against public property records, and we will check it line by line.]
- That Cantor Fitzgerald and BGC were "involved in crime and money laundering," with the "Russian Mafia" mentioned as one possible source of alleged laundered funds. [S — recorded as an allegation.]
- That Lutnick provided office space to Sarah Ferguson's "Children in Crisis" charity, and that Maxwell and Ferguson hosted "La Dolce Vita Parties" to which Lutnick "donated huge." [S — recorded as an allegation.]

Now — the grading, stated plainly, because this document's rule requires it at the front and not in a footnote. The FBI wrote these things down. The FBI did not verify them. And the finest independent forensic analysis of the released files — the open-source investigation community that has spent thousands of hours cross-referencing the Epstein document trove — graded his tip this way: the reporter appeared to be "a former employee or contractor who had a negative experience," making claims "in multiple forums (FBI, IRS)," and "the claim about the $10 house sale is the most specific and verifiable allegation," while "the broader Ponzi scheme / money laundering allegations are unsubstantiated in the FBI files," and — this is the sentence to hold onto — "no follow-up investigation notes were found in the databases." [S-adjacent — the rhowardstone Epstein-research dossier, whose author verified every claim against the underlying documents.]

Also, and this must be said with equal weight: in the interview itself, Andriesz told the FBI he had never interacted with Lutnick and had never met Epstein or Maxwell. [S — the FD-302.] His account was not presented as firsthand observation of their meetings. It was presented as what he had seen inside the firm's machinery, and what he inferred from the documents he had.

So Family One and Family Two stand on different ground before we have examined a single other document. Family One's core — the commission pools, the share scheme, the reporting failures — has a documented regulatory aftermath that we will trace in Part VII: penalties, awards, and tax assessments at three different agencies in two countries. Family Two's criminal core — the money-laundering and Ponzi claims — produced one formal regulatory credit and no criminal process anywhere, and the independent graders found it unverified.

Both families are told here in full. Neither is adopted. That is what the evidence supports, and the reader deserves to have it stated on the first page rather than discovered on the fiftieth.

## Chapter 3: The Chronology of Reporting

Here is the full sequence, in order, of what Andriesz did with what he believed he had found. Every date in this chapter is on the record.

**2015.** He suffers a heart attack at his desk. [B]

**2016.** He reports his concerns internally at BGC and, he later said, refuses to sign off on documents containing figures he regarded as false. [A — Forbes/BBC.] This is the year he dates the beginning of his whistleblowing.

**2017.** BGC terminates his employment on January 31, 2017. [S — the federal arbitration record.] His account: the termination was pretextual, punishment for the internal reports, wrapped in a dispute over his medical fitness — BGC ordered him to submit to examinations by two doctors of the firm's choosing, and then dismissed him. [S — same record.] BGC's account, on the record: he had an axe to grind, refused to follow medical advice, failed to perform his duties. [A — Forbes.] Both accounts are preserved; the tribunal and arbitration machinery that heard them never ruled on the underlying merits, and we will see exactly why.

Also in 2017: he begins reporting to the UK's tax authority, HMRC, about partnership tax arrangements at the firm. [S-adjacent — the later tax tribunal record references "a substantial fraud perpetrated by former employees" affecting the very arrangements he says he reported.]

**2020.** In October, he phones the FBI's National Threat Operations Center from the United Kingdom. [S] The intake report — we will read it shortly — records a caller from the UK who had worked in New York from 2015 to 2017, who said he had documentation he was willing to provide, and who said that raising his concerns had caused him to have a heart attack and seven subsequent hospitalizations. [S]

**February 16, 2021.** The FBI interviews him by telephone. An agent writes up the interview on a form called an FD-302 — the FBI's standard record of an interview. [S] We will walk through this document page by page in Chapter 4, because it is the foundation stone of this entire file, and because the way it entered the public record is itself one of the story's most important episodes.

**2020–2021.** He pursues regulators on both sides of the Atlantic. In the United States, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — the federal agency that oversees derivatives markets — formally recognizes him as a whistleblower. [S — the record includes a "CFTC Preliminary Determination to grant Andriesz a Whistleblower Award," filed as an exhibit in his own later court case.] In the UK, he reports to the Financial Conduct Authority, beginning a disclosure relationship that the FCA's own later review confirms ran from at least 2017 to 2026. [S — FCA.]

**2024.** He petitions a federal court in New York to vacate a FINRA arbitration award that had gone against him — his employment case against BGC. The judge dismisses the petition: it was filed too late, and the court found no legal basis to disturb the award. [S — Judge Lewis J. Liman, Andriesz v. BGC Financial, L.P., April 23, 2025.]

**2025.** In August, an employment tribunal in London strikes out his UK case against BGC Partners, Howard Lutnick personally, and his former supervisor, ruling it filed years out of time. [S] The ruling is about timing, not truth — the judge never reached the merits — but it lands as the closing of the last door through which a British court might have examined the allegations, and it lands thirteen days before his death.

**January 2026.** The U.S. Department of Justice, complying with the Epstein Files Transparency Act, releases millions of pages of documents from its Epstein files. Andriesz searches them — he has explained his method publicly: he searched for the initials "HWL," knowing executives correspond by initials — and finds what he is looking for: a 2018 email exchange between Howard Lutnick's email account and Jeffrey Epstein's, discussing a company called AdFin. [A — BBC/Yahoo.] His own FBI file, it turns out, was released in the same wave, his surname visible where the redaction should have hidden it. [A — Narativ.]

**February 2026.** He begins passing what he has found to journalists and to members of the U.S. Congress. [A]

**July 14, 2026.** The BBC broadcasts its File on 4 investigation, "Epstein Files: Lutnick, the Royals and the British Whistleblower," built around his findings. [A] He is, at last, named on British national radio — though the broadcast itself withholds his full identity, and the public naming comes months later.

**August 2026.** He creates a social media account under his own name. His first statement: "I am the British whistleblower who reported evidence concerning Howard Lutnick, his links to Jeffrey Epstein, and Cantor Fitzgerald and BGC to the FBI in 2020-21. I later shared evidence with the House Oversight Committee... My disclosures also contributed to regulatory action in the US and UK, including a £96m HMRC tax assessment relating to BGC's partnership scheme. I believe the true tax exposure is substantially higher." [B — his account of record.] On August 9 he posts what will be his final public words: "The personal consequences of whistleblowing, and the retaliation I say followed, have been devastating for my family and me." He announces he intends to "share more of the evidence and the story behind what happened." He never posts again. [A — Forbes.]

**September 23, 2026.** He is found dead in Khlong Luang, a town in Pathum Thani province, just north of Bangkok, in the morning. The local municipality records the cause of death as "asphyxiation from hanging." [B — the expatriate press record, consistent with the BBC, The Banker, and the Hindustan Times.] He is fifty-seven.

**October 2026.** His death becomes public. On October 5, the Telegraph names him — "British Epstein whistleblower found dead" — and the coverage cascades: Forbes, the Daily Mail, Quartz, the BBC, The Banker, trade press on both sides of the Atlantic, and within days, the Chinese-language internet ("another 'suicide,'" as one headline put it) and the American congressional record. [A/B — the naming-day layer is itself documented in Part XIII, because of how fast unverified details propagated.]

**October 10, 2026.** Two and a half weeks after his death, an American podcast host releases an episode recorded before it, titled "I'm Not Suicidal." [B — podcast aggregators confirm the title, the date, and the fifty-three-minute length.] Part V is devoted to what we know and do not know about that episode.

**November 2, 2026.** The Financial Conduct Authority publishes the terms of reference for an independent review of how it handled his disclosures. [S] Part VI is devoted to that review — and to everything the terms leave out.

That is the chronology. Now, the foundation document.

## Chapter 4: The FBI's Write-Up — Reading the FD-302

When the FBI interviews someone, the agent afterward writes a report on a form called an FD-302. It is not a transcript; it is the agent's summary of what was said, written in the third person. Two things about every 302 matter to a reader: what it records, and what it is. What it records is the interviewee's account. What it is — and this is printed at the bottom of the Andriesz document itself — is neither recommendations nor conclusions of the FBI. It is "the property of the FBI and is loaned to your agency... not to be distributed outside your agency." [S — the document's own footer.]

Andriesz's 302 exists. It is three pages long. It carries the FBI's case number for the Epstein investigation and a serial number — Serial 593 — and it was drafted on March 17, 2021, a month after the February 16 phone interview. [S — EFTA01249207, in the Justice Department's DataSet 9 release, page one verified line by line against the public document in November 2026.] It also carries a marking that tells you its history: "SUBJECT TO PROTECTIVE ORDER, PARAGRAPHS 7, 8, 9, 10, 15, and 17" — meaning that when it was created, a court had ordered the Epstein materials kept under seal, and the document was never supposed to circulate as it now does. [S]

Page one, in the agent's rendering, establishes who he is and what he saw. Thirty-two years in securities. BGC from 2012 to 2017, London then New York. The Series 30 license — he "supervised regulatory reporting and held the most senior position in his unit," managing fifty people. [S] Then the substance: the home-access "rope traders," the overridden controls, the commission pools being "robbed" to cover losses, with the request coming to him because his signature sat on the regulatory filings. And the share scheme: ten percent of salary and bonus into shares that never vested, sellable only with a four-year re-commitment, with no voting rights, while the price fell. [S]

Pages two and three carry the Lutnick-Epstein material quoted in Chapter 2 — the "ran everything" and "smokescreen" allegations, the charity-day fraud claim, the $10 house claim, the Russian-mafia speculation, the Ferguson office-space claim. [S — as recorded in the document table of the independent Lutnick investigation, which verified the document against the corpus. The released pages are image-scans that resist machine extraction, so our line-by-line verification covers page one, while pages two and three are confirmed through that independent forensic layer.]

Now the three-layer grading, because everything in this file depends on holding all three layers at once.

**Layer one — what the documents are.** A real FD-302. A real intake report from October 2020. A real vetting memo from January 2025. All three exist in the government's own release, with serial numbers and stamps. [S]

**Layer two — what they record.** That Andriesz reported these things, and that the FBI's database searches turned up twenty-six case-file references touching Lutnick, including a restricted closed money-laundering case file and a RICO real-estate control file, and three suspicious-activity reports filed by banks. [S — recorded in a companion document to the 302, dated April 2021.] A database hit is a pointer, not a finding: it means a name appeared in a record, not that the record accuses the person of anything. The independent investigators who examined this exact document were careful to say precisely that, and this file repeats their care: "database-search result, not a statement of Lutnick's role in the underlying records." [S-adjacent — GAH investigation.]

**Layer three — what they prove.** Here the floor drops out, and honesty requires standing on the dropped floor. The allegations were not verified. The independent forensic dossier says so in as many words: the broader Ponzi and money-laundering claims are "unsubstantiated in the FBI files." No follow-up investigation notes exist in the released databases. The January 2025 vetting memo records the bureau's decision in one flat sentence of declination — quoted in full in the next chapter, where the confirmation-window timeline belongs. [S]

Nothing in layer one or layer two rescues layer three, and nothing in layer three erases layers one or two. A man reported specific, checkable claims to the FBI in 2020 and 2021. The bureau recorded them, ran name searches, declined to open an investigation, and said so in writing during Lutnick's confirmation-period vetting — by which point Lutnick was being considered for a cabinet seat. Whether the declination was sound is a question this file poses and cannot answer from the public record; the underlying case files that would allow the question to be answered have not been released. What the record shows is the shape: specific allegations, database hits, no investigation, no documented reasoning beyond the one sentence.

There is no documented indication in the released record that the Senate committee weighing his nomination saw the tip, the 302, or the case-file hits. # PART III — THE REGULATORS: WHAT WAS SUBSTANTIATED, WHAT WAS NOT

Andriesz took his claims to five different authorities in two countries. What each of them did is the middle of this story, and it is told here institution by institution, in full, because the pattern of the whole file lives in the differences between them.

## Chapter 6: The CFTC — The One That Paid Him

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission is the American regulator for futures and derivatives markets — the markets BGC's desks traded. Its whistleblower program works like this: a person with original information that leads to a successful enforcement action can receive between ten and thirty percent of the money collected.

On November 22, 2019, the CFTC fined BGC Financial, L.P. three million dollars for supervision, recordkeeping, and reporting failures spanning 2014 to March 2019. [S — CFTC Press Release 8083-19.] The details, preserved because they matter: in 2016 the firm lost nearly four months of voice recordings covering thousands of block trades; it was slow to produce audit trails; its chief compliance officer's annual reports failed to disclose material noncompliance; and it failed to tell the CFTC about other regulators' investigations — including a Securities and Exchange Commission probe it disclosed only after settling that one for $1.25 million. [S — the same release, and SEC Press Release 2018-134 for the audio-deletion settlement.]

In September 2020, the CFTC announced awards to two whistleblowers; one was an overseas claimant whose first-hand information about "ongoing fraud and efforts to avoid CFTC detection" contributed significantly to a successful enforcement action. [S — Press Release 8239-20.] Andriesz's camp states he was that claimant; the Scottish Financial News reported the award at $420,000, and reported him saying the payout was absorbed by legal costs and more than $200,000 in medical bills. [A/B] In his own subsequent litigation, a court exhibit exists bearing the title "CFTC Preliminary Determination to grant Andriesz a Whistleblower Award" — the grant itself, in the court record. [S] The CFTC never confirms whistleblower identities by statute, so the public announcement cannot be tied to him by the announcement alone; the court exhibit and his own account are the record, and they are the record this file stands on.

The grading: the CFTC action is S-tier and undisputed. The linkage of his tip to the action is established to the level of a formal preliminary determination in his favor, a matching award announcement, and his own account — A/B-tier as to the exact amount, S-tier as to the grant's existence. The commission credited him; it did not adjudicate his claims. A whistleblower award says the information contributed to an enforcement outcome. It does not say the information was proven. That distinction holds everywhere in this file.

One coda for completeness: in September 2026 — a month before his death became public — the CFTC adopted a final rule presuming the maximum thirty percent award for whistleblowers in cases collecting five million dollars or less. [S — effective October 16, 2026.] The program that paid him $420,000 now starts future small-award claimants at the maximum. His case did not drive the rulemaking, and this file does not claim it did; the timing is recorded because the program he gave three years of his life to continued without him.

## Chapter 7: The SEC — The Overlap Nobody Emphasizes

On September 30, 2020 — ten months after his FBI interview, weeks after the CFTC award — the Securities and Exchange Commission fined BGC Partners $1.4 million. [S — SEC Release 33-10867.] The charge: between the first quarter of 2015 and the first quarter of 2016 — the exact span of Andriesz's BGC employment and the beginning of his internal complaints — BGC's non-GAAP profit measure, "Post-Tax DE," was materially inflated in its public releases, by more than thirty percent in its 2015 year-end statement. The mechanism: expenses were excluded from the pre-tax figure while their tax deductions were taken — having the deduction without the expense. The expenses included, per the legal analysis of the order, a lawsuit settlement, partnership-unit expenses, and charitable contributions, including a $100 million legal settlement whose deduction was claimed without deducting the settlement from the headline number. [S — the order; A — Cleary Gottlieb's client note.]

Read that again slowly, because it is the closest the official record comes to the substance of what Andriesz said he saw: during the period of his employment and complaint, the firm's headline profitability measure was officially found to be misleading, in part because of how it handled partnership-unit expenses and settlement costs — the same categories as the commission pools and diversion claims at the heart of his account.

The grading, with the discipline this file owes its reader: the SEC order does not credit Andriesz as its source, and no public document ties his tip to the action. What is documented is the factual overlap — same period, same subject categories, same firm. Whether his information contributed is unknown from the public record. Whether the overlap is coincidence cannot be determined from the public record. What is S-tier is the finding itself: BGC's numbers were misleading, in public releases, while he was there and complaining.

## Chapter 8: HMRC — The Nine-Digit Determination

In the United Kingdom, His Majesty's Revenue and Customs opened a compliance review in July 2016 into whether members of BGC's UK partnership should be taxed as employees under the "salaried member rules." [S — the tax tribunal record.] Andriesz's account is that he had been reporting these arrangements to HMRC since 2017. [B — his camp's claim.]

On March 8, 2024, HMRC issued determinations totaling £96,037,893 against BGC Services Holdings LLP for the tax years 2017-18 through 2019-20 — without, the tribunal later found, providing reasons as it was legally required to do. [S — BGC Services Holdings LLP v. Revenue and Customs, [2025] UKFTT 700 (TC), Judge Anne Redston, June 9, 2025.] BGC appealed; the tribunal ordered HMRC to properly state its case and refused the revenue's request to appeal further. [S] BGC's own securities filings then disclosed more: a further determination for 2021-22, national-insurance proceedings for 2017-18 through 2021-22, and a total accrued liability of $40.6 million after an additional $24.5 million accrual in the second quarter of 2026 — which the company, on the record, continues to contest. [S — BGC Group Form 10-Q, August 10, 2026.]

So: a nine-figure tax dispute over the exact species of arrangement he says he reported, formally documented, contested by the company, unresolved at his death.

The grading, stated fully: the determinations are S-tier. The tribunal's procedural rulings are S-tier. That the arrangement he reported is the one HMRC challenged is established by the correspondence of subject matter and his camp's documentation. # PART IV — THE FCA: THE INSTITUTION THAT RECEIVED HIM

If this file has a center of gravity, it is here. One British regulator received Andriesz's disclosures across nine years — 2017 to 2026 — and what it did and did not do is documented now in its own records, its own statistics, its own apologies, and the terms of the review it commissioned after his death. This part walks through the machinery, the numbers, the errors, and the review, in full, without editorializing. The numbers editorialize on their own.

## Chapter 10: How the Machine Works

Here is the Financial Conduct Authority's whistleblowing process, from its own published description. [S — fca.org.uk.]

A report arrives — by web form, email, phone, or letter. The Whistleblowing Team turns it into an "anonymized intelligence report." The team, on the record, "manages the relationship with the whistleblower. However, we do not carry out the investigations. We pass the intelligence to the relevant area of the FCA who are considered subject matter experts." [S] The receiving area assesses and investigates. The Whistleblowing Team feeds the outcome back and closes the case.

Two published limits matter to this story. First: "If the intelligence received is limited in detail and non-specific, we will upload the report to the FCA's searchable intelligence system to inform us in future regulatory interactions with the relevant firm." [S] There is a tier where a disclosure does not trigger action; it becomes a note in a database to be consulted the next time the firm is examined. Andriesz's camp believed his disclosures landed, at best, one tier up from that: the FCA's own public claim, after his death, is that it acted on his information and "pointed to a supervision order it had issued as a result of his allegations." [A/B — GB News quoting an FCA spokesman.] His camp's characterization, on the record: "a lower-tier regulatory measure that required changes to the firm's practices but carried none of the financial consequences... It also did nothing to address the retaliation." [B — Transparency Task Force.] The supervision order itself — the document — has not been published, and a freedom-of-information request for it is outstanding; both characterizations of the same instrument are preserved here, tagged, with the document itself named as the open receipt that would settle the description.

Second limit: "We cannot provide regular substantive updates... or be specific on how long the process will take." [S] The whistleblower reports; the machine processes; the whistleblower waits, often years, with no timeline.

Now the funnel, from the FCA's own statistics. In 2019 the FCA received 1,179 whistleblowing reports containing 2,747 allegations; it received 1,075 reports in 2020 and 1,025 in 2021. The published figures for those years show the machine's throughput: in 2019, fifty-nine matters led to what the FCA calls significant action; in 2020, forty-six; in 2021, seven. [S — FCA annual whistleblowing reports.] By April 2021, of the 2019 disclosures, 863 had been assessed and 316 were still under assessment — sixteen months after receipt; of the 2020 disclosures, only 445 had been assessed, with 630 unassessed. [S — FOI release 8157.] In the first quarter of 2026 — the quarter in which he died — the FCA received 355 new reports, up twenty-six percent year on year; 265 were closed; significant action followed in twenty-three. [S — FCA Q1 2026 data.]

Hold the shape of that funnel in mind: on the order of thirteen hundred reports a year, of which roughly forty-odd — three percent, then less than one percent, in the years on record — produce significant action. Andriesz's disclosures were a few rows in that intake. That is not an accusation; it is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the FCA's own.

And here is the statistic that this file submits is the most important number in the entire institution: the FCA's published figures count reports, allegations, assessments, actions. As one careful commentary put it — in language this file adopts — "the published whistleblowing statistics reviewed do not separately tell us how many people were protected from retaliation, what protective interventions were made, or whether they succeeded." [B — Academy of Life Planning.] Information can be useful to a regulator while the person providing it remains exposed. The machine measures what it ingests. It does not measure what happens to the people who feed it.

## Chapter 11: The Error, Admitted

In 2023, the FCA told Andriesz something in writing that was wrong, and the wrongness took him seven years to get corrected.

The setting: he had come to believe that because his identity had become known to his employer, he had lost his legal protection under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 — Britain's whistleblower-shield law. The FCA's correspondence, in a decision letter routed through the Financial Regulators Complaints Commissioner in March 2023, told him effectively that he had. The Complaints Commissioner's published response, issued August 1, 2023, carried the error; the Commissioner, Amerdeep Somal, recorded that she had relied on a legal interpretation supplied by the FCA itself. The Commissioner's formal finding did not uphold his wider complaint; the complaint's failure and the regulator's error are separate facts, and this file keeps both on the table. [S-adjacent — Complaints Commissioner report 202201760, FCA reference 208125268; the FCA's own listing; multiple corroborating press accounts. The Commissioner's formal finding did not uphold his wider complaint — both facts stand: the complaint failed, and the legal advice was wrong.]

The Public Interest Disclosure Act does not condition protection on anonymity. The FCA later accepted that the advice was wrong, confirmed he had not lost his protection, apologized, and committed to guidance and training for its whistleblowing team. [A/B — Scottish Financial News; the FCA's published response.] The regulator's own general counsel put the institutional frame on the same week the story broke: the FCA had asked Lea Paterson to review the case, and "we plan to share any lessons... early next year." [A/B.]

Why does a seven-year-old clerical-level legal error deserve a full chapter? Because of what it did in the meantime. His own account, given to MPs and campaigners weeks before his death, is that the erroneous advice weakened his position in his employment fight — that he believed, on the regulator's own word, that the shield he thought he had was gone. [A/B] The correction arrived in 2023, by which time the limitation clock that would kill his tribunal claim in August 2026 had been running for years. Causation between the error and any specific outcome is his claim, tagged as such; the error itself, the correction, and the apology are documented.

## Chapter 12: Latif — The Parallel File

There is one other case in the public record where a whistleblower fought the FCA itself, and its outcome is the adversarial counterweight this file is required to carry.

"Mr A Latif" was the FCA's own freedom-of-information officer. In 2020 he uncovered that the authority had been misusing the time extensions available under freedom-of-information law for at least two and a half years; his own team's internal review confirmed it — seventeen of eighteen sampled cases had been handled inappropriately, the problem was structural, and the FCA's self-calculated compliance rate fell from ninety percent to sixty-eight percent when recalculated. [S/B — the tribunal record and contemporaneous coverage.]

He complained, declined whistleblower status at the time, was dismissed in 2021 after the discovery of his email exfiltration to a personal account, and took the FCA to an employment tribunal claiming his disclosures caused his dismissal. In August 2025, Employment Judge Park dismissed every claim — the detriment claims "not well founded," the automatic unfair dismissal claim dismissed, the ordinary unfair dismissal claim dismissed. [S — Latif v. Financial Conduct Authority, case 3200291/2023, judgment approved August 8, 2025.] The tribunal heard fifteen FCA witnesses; it accepted that the dismissal process followed the email discovery; it found no evidence that the deciding manager even knew of the whistleblowing. The FCA won on every count.

This file records that outcome at full weight, because the honest ledger demands it: the one tribunal-tested whistleblower-retaliation case against the FCA lost on every ground. What survives the loss is the underlying wrongdoing the tribunal did not dispute — the FOI misuse was real, confirmed by the FCA's own review — but on the question the Andriesz chapter keeps asking, the Latif judgment is the system's best defense: when the FCA's process was tested in court, it was found to have operated as designed.

## Chapter 14: The Wider Reform Window

Andriesz's death landed inside the largest whistleblowing-policy moment in Britain in a generation, and this file records the juxtaposition without claiming he caused it.

On October 12, 2026 — nineteen days after his death — the government opened a formal consultation on overhauling the whistleblowing framework, the first comprehensive review in twenty-five years. [S — "A consultation on whistleblowing," Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade, responses due January 8, 2027.] The government's own diagnosis, in its words: the framework is "too reactive" — remedies after the damage, not prevention; "fragmented" — over ninety "prescribed persons" receive disclosures, organized by organization rather than by type of wrongdoing; and lacking "central oversight." ## Chapter 25: The House Next Door — the $10 Deed Chain, Resolved

The most famous single claim in Andriesz's tip — "Epstein sold Lutnick a home for $10 which was then sold for millions" — is also the most checkable, and the checking is a small masterpiece of what public records can and cannot do. Here is the chain, every step of it on file with the City of New York.

Epstein's mansion was 9 East 71st Street in Manhattan. The house next door, 11 East 71st, has a documented ownership history that runs like this. In 1988, a corporation called SAM Conversion Corporation — registered at Les Wexner's Columbus, Ohio address, with Epstein listed as its vice president on the deed — bought the property. In December 1992, SAM Conversion sold it to a trust whose trustee was Jeffrey Epstein: "Jeffrey E. Epstein, Trustee, 11 East 71st Street Trust." The recorded consideration on both of these transfers, and on the next one, was ten dollars — the nominal figure standardly entered when property moves between related entities. In April 1996 the Epstein trust sold to the Comet Trust, a vehicle associated with the de Gunzburg and Bronfman fortunes, again for ten recorded dollars, with transfer taxes of $86,800 indicating a real price of roughly $6.2 million. In January 1998, the Comet Trust sold to Howard W. Lutnick — the deed says ten dollars; the transfer taxes of $106,400 indicate a real price of roughly $7.6 million, financed in part by a $4 million mortgage taken the same day. [S/A — the New York property records as reported by Crain's New York in 2019 and confirmed across the record.]

So the "$10 house" claim decomposes into three parts, and each part gets its own grade. Was there a $10 deed? Yes — recorded, in the chain above, three times. Was the actual price $10? No — the transfer taxes disclose real consideration in the millions; the $10 entries are the standard nominal consideration of trust-chain transfers, and Lutnick's purchase was at market, financed with a mortgage. Was there a substantively disturbing fact underneath? Yes, and it is this: Lutnick bought his family home out of a chain in which Jeffrey Epstein was the trustee, and the trust he bought from continued to receive mail addressed to Epstein at that address as late as 2005, after the Lutnicks had moved in. [S — the 2005 fax cover sheet in the released files, addressed to "Jeffrey E. Epstein" at "11 East 71st Street Trust."] Under oath, Lutnick said he bought the house from "someone named Charles de Gunzburg," did not know and did not think he had ever known that Epstein had owned it, and accepted as new information, that day in the deposition, the fact of Epstein's trusteeship in the chain. An early critique argued that because the deed chain does not appear in the FBI's released Epstein corpus, the $10 claim was likely a conflation.
